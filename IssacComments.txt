General comments:
	Ch. 1
		I found the introduction to lack a bit of broader context. Starting by saying what the Standard Model says elevates it above its status as "a very descriptive theory" to almost a set of religious laws. It would help to mention that the Standard Model has been validated extensively with tests of particle production and interactions, across a very broad range of energy, etc., but that a) there are questions of internal consistency, and b) we know that there are things it does not/cannot describe, e.g. dark matter/energy, neutrino masses, etc. 
		Another lack of context is in the description of interaction vertices on pgs. 1, 2. This implicitly introduces the paradigm of perturbative quantum field theory and Feynman diagrams without explicitly saying so. This represents a single (very successful and helpful) way of organizing our understanding and arriving at results, but it is not the only, or only valid, way of approaching problems, especially as we will see the shortfalls of the perturbative approach in the coming sections with the impact of non-perturbative QCD in the experimental regime of the experiment discussed.
		There is also a problem of a lack of care for the audience of the thesis. For example, the description of the QCD Lagrangian assumes a knowledge of the quantum property of color. This raises the question of what reader would be familiar with QCD color, but unfamiliar with the most basic thing about QCD: its Lagrangian. I would recommend giving serious thought to who is meant to read this document -- should the introduction at least be accessible to any adult? Any physicist of any level of education? Any professional particle/nuclear physicist? I won't impose my own view (which is along the lines of: the thesis should start from the most general possible level such that almost anyone can read the first few pages to have a sense of where your topic of study fits within humanity's overall understanding of nature, and gradually by the end filter down to experts in your domain of study (and motivated students in that field looking for information, and committee members evaluating your work)); my point is that it should at least be consistent, and have some idea of who it is speaking to. 
		There is a lack of restraint/tact in what is included. Pgs. 9 - 14 largely fell into the category of: it's very good to know, but better once the material is understood to simply claim a given useful fact for the narrative of the thesis, and cite resources as necessary. There just isn't enough space allotted here to re-derive all of the important concepts in pQCD, so the resulting discussion feels very slapdash. An interested reader can follow the citation and read the derivations there, while the one who either knows it already or is more interested to see the work that you did can more quickly get to that without wondering if particular intermediate steps will become important later. 
		Finally, I don't feel the studies are currently motivated enough -- we finish the first chapter with a description of Monte Carlo, then the outline, but nowhere do I feel it currently mentions why the studies performed for this thesis were an important contribution to the field without which we would understand less. Toward the end of the last section it mentions "prob[ing] the dependence of energy loss on the path length traversed in the medium" -- this would be a good place to start for a motivation but currently it's just thrown out there with no justification. And in general, jets as a hard probe are not sufficiently motivated -- the QGP is mentioned in less than a page, and jets as useful tomographic probes are not really mentioned at all. This would be okay if the thesis were only on pp collisions, but as you'll show heavy-ion results, this should really be rectified.

		pg. 6 -- You are not alone -- I have seen this incorrect logic in every thesis I've ever edited: "low-energy => coupling diverges => confinement" is improper. The coupling does not diverge: the determination of the coupling from perturbative QCD diverges. But when the coupling is large enough, we should no longer be using perturbative QCD to tell us anything about nature! We must then switch our thinking to non-perturbative QCD, which tells us that the coupling should actually flatten at some low Q value (as it must, because in nature we don't really get divergences [another way of saying this would be that the Landau pole is unphysical] -- e.g. the proton has a finite size so the coupling cannot diverge at large distances). [See e.g. https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.08082 -- I highly recommend reading as much of this as you can get through as it is extremely insightful and helps retrain your brain away from using perturbative hammers as the only tool in your toolbox even when problems are not nails but instead screws.] Anyway, what this means is that the first logical link is broken. What happens to the second one? Well we now have a finite coupling, and yet still also confinement. So something else must be going on. In the article I link above they also point out that "For example the coupling is finite in QCD(1+1) which is a confining theory." So it is not necessary that one have a diverging coupling to produce a confining theory.
